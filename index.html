Abstract

A hallmark of human dexterity is the cooperative use of fingers, where different fingers take on distinct yet coordinated roles to accomplish fine manipulation, such as capping a pen with the hand that holds it. We study this finger-level coordination through in-hand assembly: mating two rigid objects within a single dexterous hand, with no second arm and no fixture. We present a reinforcement learning formulation to solve this problem in a unified framework, which is driven by a goal relative pose between the two parts. Finger coordination is shaped by a function-based auxiliary reward and regularized toward a single human reference pose, while domain randomization and a fusion of historical proprioception and object observation confer robustness to occlusion-induced estimation noise. The same recipe solves three different assembly tasks (Bottle, Syringe, and Marker). Trained purely in simulation, the policies transfer zero-shot to hardware with a single camera, demonstrating robustness to state-estimation errors caused by occlusion. Our experiments also reveal that in-hand assembly places demands on hand morphology, as a benchmark for modern robotic hand systems.

Overview

In-hand assembly requires one hand to support both objects while bringing them into a target relative pose. The task family includes bottle-cap assembly, syringe insertion, and marker-cap fitting. Each task demands precise alignment and coordinated contact across multiple fingers.

Our real-world system uses a 22-DoF Sharpa Wave Hand, mounted on a Franka Research 3 arm, and a single RealSense D435 camera. FoundationPose tracks the objects, while a recurrent policy combines their estimated poses with the hand's joint-angle history to control assembly.

Method

We formulate assembly as a partially observable reinforcement learning problem driven by the relative pose between two parts. A shared observation, action, and reward formulation is used across the three tasks.

  1. Relative-pose goal reaching. Position and axis-alignment rewards guide the held part toward the assembly target on the other part.
  2. Functional finger coordination. Auxiliary rewards encourage the thumb and index finger to manipulate one part while the remaining fingers support and coordinate the other. A single human reference snapshot provides an initialization and a nominal hand-pose prior.
  3. Robust closed-loop control. Recurrent policies combine estimated object states with proprioceptive history. Domain randomization and a depth-consistency gate help address noisy observations caused by occlusion during deployment.

Real-World Results

We compare the closed-loop policy with open-loop replay of successful simulation trajectories in 20 consecutive real-world trials per task and method. Feedback substantially improves both alignment and completed assembly.

Robustness and Hand Morphology

We evaluate policies under random observation noise and systematic offsets in simulation. Real-world tests include externally induced cap misalignment, pulling the syringe plunger out during execution, and assembly at different hand tilts. These experiments demonstrate recovery from some unexpected state deviations, while also exposing limits under large estimation offsets and challenging grasp configurations.

In-hand assembly also probes the capabilities of different robotic hands. Experiments on Sharpa, Wuji, Allegro, and XHand in simulation show that finger count, joint ranges, and available degrees of freedom affect precise alignment and deep insertion.

Limitations & Future Work

The current setting starts with objects already in the hand; it does not include picking up multiple objects and arranging them for assembly. Some experiments require initial support until the policy establishes contact. The task suite currently covers three plug-in assemblies, with more general geometries, threading, and friction-fit assembly left for future work.

Observed failures include unfavorable initial cap poses, fingers obstructing the insertion path, and unstable pinch configurations at certain hand tilts. Differences between simulated contact and real soft finger pads remain a source of sim-to-real error.
